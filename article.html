The New Cartography of Influence: How AI Is Redrawing Geopolitical Borders

State-backed language models are reshaping information ecosystems across six continents — and Western intelligence agencies are only beginning to map the terrain.
M
Meera Iyer
Senior Correspondent, Tech & Geopolitics · March 26, 2026 · Updated 3h ago
Visualization of state-sponsored AI content operations mapped across global communication networks. Illustration: The Signal / Data: CISA, NCSC

In a nondescript office block in the eastern suburbs of Shanghai, a team of approximately 200 engineers is engaged in work that no press release will ever describe. They are training language models — not to answer customer queries or write marketing copy, but to understand how narratives spread, fracture, and mutate across the fractured archipelago of global social media.

This is, according to four people with direct knowledge of the project who spoke to The Signal on condition of anonymity, one of at least eleven such state-linked programs currently active across China, Russia, Iran, and three additional nations that intelligence officials declined to name. The goal is not crude propaganda. It is something far more subtle, far more durable, and far more difficult to counter.

"The old model was to shout louder than everyone else. The new model is to make everyone else uncertain about what they're hearing."

— Senior official, GCHQ, speaking on background

The Architecture of Epistemic Warfare

The shift began, most analysts agree, sometime between 2022 and 2024, when large language models became capable enough to produce text indistinguishable from human-written content at scale. The change this enabled was not merely quantitative — more disinformation produced faster — but qualitative. For the first time, influence operations could be responsive, adaptive, and contextually sophisticated in ways no human team could match.

Traditional influence operations worked by inserting false content into information ecosystems and hoping it would spread. The new approach is different: it involves identifying the specific cognitive vulnerabilities of target populations — their existing suspicions, their trusted voices, their known blind spots — and crafting narratives precisely calibrated to exploit them.

The signals intelligence community has a term for this: epistemic warfare. It is not about making people believe false things — that is too easily detected and countered. It is about making people uncertain about true things. The target is not belief but trust itself.

What the Models Are Actually Doing

The technical architecture varies by nation-state actor, but certain patterns have emerged. Based on leaked procurement documents, academic publications from affiliated research institutes, and interviews with former intelligence officials, The Signal has reconstructed a general picture of how these systems operate.

📌 Key Finding

State-backed AI influence operations are not primarily producing fake news. They are producing authentic-seeming commentary that amplifies existing divisions within target societies — a far harder problem to detect and counter.

The core capability is what researchers call "narrative injection" — the ability to introduce a framing device into public discourse that, once established, shapes how subsequent genuine information is interpreted. A story about a political scandal, for instance, might be preceded by weeks of subtle AI-generated commentary establishing the conditions under which the scandal will seem most damning.

The Western Response — and Its Limits

Western governments are not passive observers. The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, Britain's NCSC, and the European Union's External Action Service have all expanded their counter-influence capabilities significantly since 2024. Several democratic nations have established joint task forces specifically focused on AI-enabled information operations.

But there is a fundamental asymmetry that officials find difficult to discuss publicly. Authoritarian states can deploy these capabilities with minimal legal or ethical constraints. Democratic governments, by contrast, face significant barriers — both legal and political — to the kind of domestic counter-messaging that would be most effective.

"We are defending a principle — the right of citizens to make up their own minds — using tools that would compromise that principle if we used them aggressively."

— Former NSC Director for Strategic Communications

The Road Ahead

The scholars and officials who study this problem most closely are not optimistic about near-term solutions. The underlying technology — capable, cheap, and rapidly improving — is now widely available. The knowledge required to deploy it for influence operations is spreading through technical communities that are not geographically or jurisdictionally bounded.

What is possible, most agree, is improving the resilience of democratic societies to manipulation — teaching citizens to recognize the patterns, building institutional trust through demonstrated accountability, and creating the social and technical infrastructure that makes epistemic attacks less effective. None of this is quick, cheap, or easy. But the alternative — ceding the information environment to actors who have no compunction about exploiting it — is not viable.

The cartographers of influence are drawing new maps. The question is whether democratic societies will develop the tools to read them in time.
